### CapEx

Capital expenditure (CAPEX) is spending on an asset or improvement expected to provide long-term benefit to the business. There are various ways of estimating the cost, minimising risk, and maximising the return, all before making an investment.

Expected Returns

Return on Investment
The simplest method is return on investment (ROI) which compares the total cost of the investment with the amount it earned over it’s working life. For instance, if a new $50,000 machine saves $10,000 in operating costs over a year, for 10 years of it’s working life:

ROI = ($10,000 x 10) / $50,000 = $100,000 / $50,000 = 200%

Payback Period
This is the length of time it takes to earn back on the original investment through the cumulative cost savings. For the above example, it is five years.

Discounted Cash Flow
This adjusts the ROI with the time value of money. Cash that is held (and invested) is more powerful than cash we have to wait for, for two reasons:
	1. The cash continues to earn compound interest if it is unspent.
	2. The cash can be used for short-term purchases such as inventory.
Discounted cash flow is a measure of the extra value created compared to the money invested, in today's money. In this way, it puts cash inflow and cash outflow on equal footing.

An Example
The company has done this for the new piece of equipment. Assuming interest is 5%,
	- The machine saves $10,000 a year, or $833.33 a month. At a monthly compounded interest of 5% over 10 years, this equates to $129,401.38. This is the future value of the money.
	- Because the last $833.33 saving doesn't come until 10 years in the future, it's value when it comes will be far less, because interest rates will have made what is $833.33 in 10 years time be worth $505.97 in today's money, due to inflation. Working backwards from the end until today, we calculate the savings as a discounted sum to arrive at the payback value in today's money, which is $78,567, the present value.

	- As part of its operation, the machine has maintenance costs under contract which are $15,000. However, we will pay them annually, so that we are only paying $1,500 a year and saving the rest. $1,500 this year is $1,500 in today's money, but $1,500 next year is $1,426.99 in today's money. Summing that discount over 10 years, the present value of the maintenance contract is $11,785.
	- The company is also taking out a loan to finance the equipment, but since the loan interest rate equals the savings interest rate, they cancel out and the machine still costs $50,000.

Putting that all together, 
Present value of cost savings = $78,567
Present value of maintenance contract = -$11,785
Present value of purchase = -$50,000
---
Net present value (NPV) = $16,782. So, not only do the projected savings cover the equipment and maintenance costs, they create an additional $16,782 of value in today's money.

The internal rate of return (IRR) can be calculated by setting the net present value to $0 and solving for the interest rate. In this case, it is 12.33% (thanks ChatGPT), which is much larger than 5% and makes it a good investment. The 5% comes from the finance department and is called the weighted average cost of capital (WACC). It is determined from a combination of:
	- Cash required to pay back loans, and that interest rate (cost of debts)
	- Cash required to provide equity to shareholders, and their expected dividends (cost of equity)

Risk Management
A large ROI or IRR is not the only way to determine if a project should go ahead. The risk also needs to be minimised:
	- Is the maintenance cost expected to increase over time?
	- Are there any impacts to quality, environment or safety?
	- Does the equipment require additional operating costs, utilities usage, or other consumables?
	- Will the equipment soon be superseded by something more efficient?
	- How sure are we that the machine will produce the savings expected?
	- Have we considered all possible options to determine the best one?

For the last point, a sensitivity analysis can be performed which calculates the NPV while changing one assumption (maintenance costs, savings, etc.) and keeping the others fixed. It reveals which assumptions could overturn the decision. 

Competing Priorities
Determining which capital project to proceed with is also related to a variety of competing priorities:
	- Consequence of deferral, such as safety, legal or regulatory reasons.
	- Demonstration of confidence that the equipment is fit for purpose.
	- Strategic fit for product mix, NPD, sustainability targets, or future production requirements.
	- Dependence on other projects being completed.
	- Availability of line downtime, supporting infrastructure, and people resources.

Project Execution and Acceptance
The brewing manager should be aware of any risks in delivery or cost of the new project. They also need to be involved in the acceptance plan, which includes:
	- Any training needed to use the equipment.
	- Safety and compliance inspection requirements.
	- Maintenance plans, spare parts, manuals, drawings, and OEM support.
	- Performance sign-off, that the equipment is functioning as intended, with all agreed tests and pass criteria.
	- Benefits tracking, measuring actual savings against those calculated in the business case.

### Balance Sheet Controls, Risk Management and Legal
Managing the balance sheet is part of every manager’s role. Working capital is cash tied up in day-to-day operations, including inventory. Holding more stock can protect production from shortages or supply delays, while larger purchases may secure a lower unit price. Managers must balance those benefits against storage costs, cash tied up in inventory, damage and obsolescence risk. 

Good stock controls protect business assets: stores should be secure and orderly, stock movements should be recorded, and physical stock should be counted regularly—through cycle counts, an annual count or both. Differences should be investigated. A stock obsolescence provision recognises that slow-moving, expired or unusable inventory may not be worth its recorded value. 

Fixed assets also need regular control. Review the asset register each year to confirm assets exist and that their remaining useful lives are realistic. When an asset is replaced or becomes redundant, remove it from the register and account for any write-off. Include potential write-offs when assessing capital approvals for replacement assets. 

Managers should identify significant operational and financial risks, assess their likely impact, and prepare plans to reduce or manage them. Insurance can transfer some financial risk, subject to the policy’s cover, exclusions and conditions. If an incident may lead to a claim, notify the insurer promptly, follow the policy requirements, keep evidence and supporting records, and check before arranging repairs. 

In Australia, production managers also need awareness of relevant compliance obligations. For beer excise, the ATO requires accurate records of production, stock, deliveries, duty and returns; excise calculations depend on the product’s dutiable alcohol quantity and applicable rate. Packaging obligations depend on the business and its role in the supply chain; qualifying brand owners generally need to meet the Australian Packaging Covenant framework or applicable state and territory requirements. Carbon credit obligations are not universal: the Safeguard Mechanism applies to covered facilities emitting more than 100,000 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent annually. 

Employment law awareness includes minimum entitlements under the National Employment Standards, as well as applicable awards or agreements and employer record-keeping and pay-slip obligations.

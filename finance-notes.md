### Finance Statement

**Balance Sheet**

The balance sheet shows business assets (what we own), liabilities (what we owe), and the difference between the two - shareholder equity - as of a particular date.

Assets (what we own)
	- Short-term assets are those with a lifespan of less than a year. Examples include inventory (raw materials, semi-finished goods, and finished goods), debt from customers, and cash.
	- Long-term assets are those with a lifespan of greater than one year. Examples include property, buildings, machinery, patents, stocks, vehicles, and computers. Depending on the company, these can be listed at the purchased value, with depreciation subtracted later, or at the estimated current market value.

Liabilities (what we owe)
	- Short-term liabilities include accounts payable, taxes payable, customer deposits, and dividends payable.
	- Long-term liabilities include long-term loans, pension liabilities, deferred employee compensation or deferred tax.

Shareholder Equity
	- The difference between assets and liabilities. Split in to two elements:
	1. Capital invested when business commenced. It is the cash that was physically provided to the business by investors and shareholders.
	2. Retained earnings, the cumulative profit that is reinvested in the company for future growth of the business, once dividends have been paid to the shareholders.
	- If the company conducts business in a way that increases assets without increasing liabilities by an equal amount (such as a loan on the asset, or paying cash for it), then the shareholder equity (i.e. value) of the company increases. 

**Profit & Loss (or Income) Statement**

This shows revenue and expenses over a fixed period. The statement captures the sale when the product is delivered to the customer - whether or not cash has changed hands. This is because the income already exists, either as accounts receivable or as cash. 

Costs are recorded to reflect the expense of making the particular sales in the P&L statement. For example, if 20,000 bottles of beer had been sold, the costs recorded include the money spent on the raw materials, people, overheads etc. to produce those bottles of beer. If the beer has not been sold, it remains on the balance sheet as a short-term asset.

From sales revenue, the cost of goods sold (COGs) is deducted. Brewing primarily influences this manufacturing cost. The figure that remains is gross profit.

From gross profit, administrative and people expenses are deducted, resulting in the EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortisation.)

From EBITDA, interest and taxes (inc. excise and GST) are deducted, and this is the net earnings figure which is the profit that exists as shareholder equity, which can be paid as dividends, re-invested, or both.

**Cashflow Statement**

Cash flow statements show how cash has been used and generated over a specific period. The flow of cash is presented using three categories:
	1. Operating cashflows including flows to and from the core operations of the business and driven by trading. These include cash from sales, cash used for staff wages, raw material purchases, and interest and tax payments.
	2. Investing cashflows deal with investments in the future of the business, such as new assets or equipment.
	3. Financing cashflows including any investments made by shareholders and dividends paid to them, as well as business borrowing or repayments of existing loans.

A negative on a cashflow statement is not necessarily profit or loss. For example, purchase of raw materials (working capital) or equipment (capital expenditure) becomes an asset on the balance sheet.

**Example #1**

The brewery buys a new centrifuge valued at $1m, that depreciates by $100k per year.

When the product is purchased:
	- Cashflow Statement will show an immediate flow of cash out equal to -$1m.
	- Balance Sheet will show -$1m in short-term assets (cash) and +$1m in long-term assets (equipment)
	- P&L Statement will show no change.

As depreciation occurs year on year:
	- P&L Statement will show a yearly $100k depreciation expense.
	- Balance Sheet will change the long-term asset's value from $1m to $900k
	- Cashflow Statement will show no change.

If the new centrifuge lowers some operational costs, such as maintenance, extract, or electricity, this will reflect on the P&L statement by reducing expenses.

**Example #2**

The brewery buys 1,000 tonnes of malt worth $700k and uses 600 t in the first month.

When the inventory is purchased:
	- Cashflow Statement will show an immediate flow of cash out equal to -$700k as the supplier is paid.
	- Balance Sheet will show -$700k in short-term assets (cash) and +$700k in other short-term assets (inventory)
	- P&L Statement will show no change.

As the beer is brewed:
	- $420k (600t / 1000t) moves through the balance sheet from raw material, to work in progress, to finished goods.

Once the corresponding quantity of beer is sold (say $60k worth of beer is sold for 120k):
	- P&L Statement will show $60k as part of the COGS and +$120k as revenue.
	- Balance Sheet will show +$120k in accounts receivable, -$60k in inventory, and +60k in equity.
	- Cashflow Statement will show $0 until the customer pays in the invoice, at which point cashflow +$120k and Balance Sheet stays almost the same, with the $120k moved from accounts receivable to cash.

**Departmental Spend Report**

	- Shows departmental costs, comparing budget vs actual vs year ago. Cents per litre (cpl) normalises cost against production volume.
  - Costs can include raw materials, utilities, labour and maintenance. If the actual cpl is higher than the budgeted cpl, the brewing manager can identify the cost category, investigate the operational cause, and proceed with corrective action.

### Budgets and Mid-Year Forecasts

**Planning Cycle**

The strategic plan sets the company’s vision, mission, strategy and longer-term objectives, typically over three years or more. It provides the direction for the annual operating plan, which turns that direction into specific goals, actions and resources for the coming year.

Goals should be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant to the strategy and trackable over time. The operating plan includes departmental plans, such as production goals, the resources needed to achieve them and the challenges that may affect delivery.

The production plan and budget connect expected sales to the production activity and resources needed to supply them. In manufacturing, a standard cost can act as the budget for making one unit of product. Budgeted material, labour and overhead costs therefore support standard costing and help inform internal prices between production and commercial operations.

**Annual Budget and Forecasts**

The annual budget translates the operating plan into financial terms. It estimates the resources the company will need and the expected financial results if the plan is carried out. It gives managers a benchmark for managing resources and measuring performance.

Budget preparation usually starts with a sales or revenue forecast, because expected demand affects production requirements and the resources other departments need. Management reviews whether the estimate is realistic, profitable and achievable with available production capacity.

During the year, actual results and updated forecasts can be compared with budget. Variance reports highlight significant differences so managers can investigate causes and take corrective action. If the company’s expected results move away from the plan, management may adjust actions, spending or targets to get delivery back on course.

Budgets should reflect real cost relationships. Costs may vary with sales or production, remain fixed, or change in steps. Other costs may be linked to a relevant driver—for example, insurance costs might be estimated as a percentage of wages. Comparing actual spending with a flexible budget can also help when activity differs from the original plan, because variable costs are recalculated for the activity level achieved.

The operating plan is supported by related budgets:
- The capital expenditure budget records planned capital purchases, their estimated cost and timing.
- The cash flow budget accounts for when cash is expected to come in and go out. A plan can be profitable but still face a cash shortage if receipts and payments are mistimed.
- An integrated budgeted balance sheet shows how the operating, capital and cash plans affect the company’s financial position and performance measures.

**Zero-based Budgeting (ZBB)**

In zero-based budgeting, each expense must be justified for the new budget period. Managers do not automatically carry forward last year’s budget and add an increase. Instead, they explain what the expense supports, how much is needed and why it should be funded.
ZBB can help challenge unnecessary or outdated spending and align resources with current priorities. It takes more time and analysis than simply adjusting previous budgets, and managers need to ensure that less easily measured areas—such as maintenance, training or long-term development—are still considered fairly. 

**Activity-Based Budgeting (ABB)**

Activity-based budgeting builds spending estimates from the work the business expects to do and the resources that work will consume. The basic logic is:
Budgeted cost = usage per activity × quantity of activity × price per unit of resource
For a brewery, that could mean estimating cleaning chemical spend from the quantity of CIP cycles, the chemical usage per cycle and the price per litre or kilogram. The same logic can be applied to materials, utilities, filter aids and other consumables. This makes the assumptions visible. If actual costs differ from budget, managers can check whether the activity quantity changed, resource usage was higher or lower than expected, or the purchase price changed. ABB can give a clearer link between operational activity and cost, though it needs reliable activity and usage data and can take more work to maintain. 

**How ZBB and ABB Fit Together**

Both approaches support better resource decisions, but they answer different questions. ZBB asks whether an expense should be funded and requires its need to be justified. ABB estimates how much resource and cost are needed for the planned activity. They can be used together: justify the activity and its purpose, then calculate its budget using usage, quantity and price.

### Standard Costs

Standard costs include all the costs of producing and storing a product. It does not include distribution, marketing, or other sales expenses. It does include:
	1. Raw materials, for brewing and packaging, called 'direct costs'.
	2. Fixed manufacturing overheads, including labour, utilities and maintenance, called 'indirect costs'.
	3. Shuttling and warehousing.

It can be used as:
	1. Internal pricing; the cost assigned to the beer as it moves through production, as each stage adds more cost (e.g. yeast, packaging materials, more utilities and labour.)
	2. Stock valuation; calculating the value of inventory on hand.

This is also referred to on the P&L as cost of goods sold (COGS), which only appear on the statement when the relevant volume of beer has been sold.

Bill of Materials (Direct Costs)
The BOM is the approved list of materials, standard quantities, and expected yield/losses for a defined amount of saleable product. These can be assigned and traced to a particular batch.

Manufacturing Overheads (Indirect Costs)
Labour, utilities, maintenance and depreciation are all collected and allocated using an agreed driver. This is a complex calculation done using financial models and incorporates production efficiencies, brewery location, age of equipment, and shift structures.

Variable and Fixed Costs
Although all costs in the long-term are variable, the distinction is about whether the cost responds to a change in volume over a particular period:
	- Variable costs change as production volume changes, for example raw materials.
	- Fixed costs stay the same as production volume changes, for example depreciation and labour.
	- Mixed or step costs are a mix of both, for example enough beer being produced that overtime is needed.

Variable and direct costs and fixed and indirect costs are very similar, however:
	- Direct/indirect is whether the cost can be traced to a particular product.
	- Variable/fixed is whether the cost changes with volume.

Using Standard Costs
Actual standard costs and variances are performed by the ERP system (e.g. SAP.)

They can be used to make brewery decisions such as:
	- Using the BOM and yield assumptions to understand the expected cost of a recipe.
	- Check whether a process change alters standard quantities, losses, or resource use.
	- Investigate repeated unfavorable variances with production and procurement data, therefore separating genuine efficiency opportunities from price movements, volume effects, or poor master data.

Variances are the differences between actual and expected costs. Costs are typically normalised against volume produced to isolate them against volume changes. Variances can occur through:
	- Price: the cost of raw materials may change due to seasonality, contracts, exchange rates, freight, surcharges, etc.
	- Efficiency: the process may have used more or less material, time, or energy to produce. Investigate yield, losses, rework, downtime and flowrates.
	- Mix: some products are more or less expensive to produce than others.
Volume: although volume is accounted for by scaling the BOM, variances can occur if the average cost is higher, for instance if less volume is produced using the same manufacturing overheads, or if more volume of an expensive product is made.

### CapEx

Capital expenditure (CAPEX) is spending on an asset or improvement expected to provide long-term benefit to the business. There are various ways of estimating the cost, minimising risk, and maximising the return, all before making an investment.

Expected Returns

Return on Investment
The simplest method is return on investment (ROI) which compares the total cost of the investment with the amount it earned over it’s working life. For instance, if a new $50,000 machine saves $10,000 in operating costs over a year, for 10 years of it’s working life:

ROI = ($10,000 x 10) / $50,000 = $100,000 / $50,000 = 200%

Payback Period
This is the length of time it takes to earn back on the original investment through the cumulative cost savings. For the above example, it is five years.

Discounted Cash Flow
This adjusts the ROI with the time value of money. Cash that is held (and invested) is more powerful than cash we have to wait for, for two reasons:
	1. The cash continues to earn compound interest if it is unspent.
	2. The cash can be used for short-term purchases such as inventory.
Discounted cash flow is a measure of the extra value created compared to the money invested, in today's money. In this way, it puts cash inflow and cash outflow on equal footing.

An Example
The company has done this for the new piece of equipment. Assuming interest is 5%,
	- The machine saves $10,000 a year, or $833.33 a month. At a monthly compounded interest of 5% over 10 years, this equates to $129,401.38. This is the future value of the money.
	- Because the last $833.33 saving doesn't come until 10 years in the future, it's value when it comes will be far less, because interest rates will have made what is $833.33 in 10 years time be worth $505.97 in today's money, due to inflation. Working backwards from the end until today, we calculate the savings as a discounted sum to arrive at the payback value in today's money, which is $78,567, the present value.

	- As part of its operation, the machine has maintenance costs under contract which are $15,000. However, we will pay them annually, so that we are only paying $1,500 a year and saving the rest. $1,500 this year is $1,500 in today's money, but $1,500 next year is $1,426.99 in today's money. Summing that discount over 10 years, the present value of the maintenance contract is $11,785.
	- The company is also taking out a loan to finance the equipment, but since the loan interest rate equals the savings interest rate, they cancel out and the machine still costs $50,000.

Putting that all together, 
Present value of cost savings = $78,567
Present value of maintenance contract = -$11,785
Present value of purchase = -$50,000
---
Net present value (NPV) = $16,782. So, not only do the projected savings cover the equipment and maintenance costs, they create an additional $16,782 of value in today's money.

The internal rate of return (IRR) can be calculated by setting the net present value to $0 and solving for the interest rate. In this case, it is 12.33% (thanks ChatGPT), which is much larger than 5% and makes it a good investment. The 5% comes from the finance department and is called the weighted average cost of capital (WACC). It is determined from a combination of:
	- Cash required to pay back loans, and that interest rate (cost of debts)
	- Cash required to provide equity to shareholders, and their expected dividends (cost of equity)

Risk Management
A large ROI or IRR is not the only way to determine if a project should go ahead. The risk also needs to be minimised:
	- Is the maintenance cost expected to increase over time?
	- Are there any impacts to quality, environment or safety?
	- Does the equipment require additional operating costs, utilities usage, or other consumables?
	- Will the equipment soon be superseded by something more efficient?
	- How sure are we that the machine will produce the savings expected?
	- Have we considered all possible options to determine the best one?

For the last point, a sensitivity analysis can be performed which calculates the NPV while changing one assumption (maintenance costs, savings, etc.) and keeping the others fixed. It reveals which assumptions could overturn the decision. 

Competing Priorities
Determining which capital project to proceed with is also related to a variety of competing priorities:
	- Consequence of deferral, such as safety, legal or regulatory reasons.
	- Demonstration of confidence that the equipment is fit for purpose.
	- Strategic fit for product mix, NPD, sustainability targets, or future production requirements.
	- Dependence on other projects being completed.
	- Availability of line downtime, supporting infrastructure, and people resources.

Project Execution and Acceptance
The brewing manager should be aware of any risks in delivery or cost of the new project. They also need to be involved in the acceptance plan, which includes:
	- Any training needed to use the equipment.
	- Safety and compliance inspection requirements.
	- Maintenance plans, spare parts, manuals, drawings, and OEM support.
	- Performance sign-off, that the equipment is functioning as intended, with all agreed tests and pass criteria.
	- Benefits tracking, measuring actual savings against those calculated in the business case.

### Balance Sheet Controls, Risk Management and Legal
Managing the balance sheet is part of every manager’s role. Working capital is cash tied up in day-to-day operations, including inventory. Holding more stock can protect production from shortages or supply delays, while larger purchases may secure a lower unit price. Managers must balance those benefits against storage costs, cash tied up in inventory, damage and obsolescence risk. 

Good stock controls protect business assets: stores should be secure and orderly, stock movements should be recorded, and physical stock should be counted regularly—through cycle counts, an annual count or both. Differences should be investigated. A stock obsolescence provision recognises that slow-moving, expired or unusable inventory may not be worth its recorded value. 

Fixed assets also need regular control. Review the asset register each year to confirm assets exist and that their remaining useful lives are realistic. When an asset is replaced or becomes redundant, remove it from the register and account for any write-off. Include potential write-offs when assessing capital approvals for replacement assets. 

Managers should identify significant operational and financial risks, assess their likely impact, and prepare plans to reduce or manage them. Insurance can transfer some financial risk, subject to the policy’s cover, exclusions and conditions. If an incident may lead to a claim, notify the insurer promptly, follow the policy requirements, keep evidence and supporting records, and check before arranging repairs. 

In Australia, production managers also need awareness of relevant compliance obligations. For beer excise, the ATO requires accurate records of production, stock, deliveries, duty and returns; excise calculations depend on the product’s dutiable alcohol quantity and applicable rate. Packaging obligations depend on the business and its role in the supply chain; qualifying brand owners generally need to meet the Australian Packaging Covenant framework or applicable state and territory requirements. Carbon credit obligations are not universal: the Safeguard Mechanism applies to covered facilities emitting more than 100,000 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent annually. 

Employment law awareness includes minimum entitlements under the National Employment Standards, as well as applicable awards or agreements and employer record-keeping and pay-slip obligations.

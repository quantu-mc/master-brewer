# Finance and Procurement

## Financial Statements
### Balance Sheet
The balance sheet shows what the company owns (assets) and what it owes (liabilites) as of a particular date.

Assets include:
- Short-term assets (<1 year), including cash, invoices owing from customers, raw materials, and product in process. 
- Long-term assets (>1 year), including equipment and property. The net value of a piece of equipment on the balance sheet will decrease over it's lifespan, with the depreciation, usually per year, accounted for in the P&L statement.

Liabilities are also divided in to short- and long-term, covering loans, debts, bills etc.

It is important to note that the purchase of a new piece of equipment is not recorded as an immediate expense. It will appear as an asset, while the equivalent amount will be either subtracted from the cash reserves (reduced asset) or added to the debt amount (increased liability), with a net equity difference of zero.

Shareholder equity is the difference between assets and liabilities. It is made up of a sum of the initial investment by shareholders, plus the cumulative profit of the business, less any dividends paid to shareholders. Shareholder equity in the company increases when the company conducts business in a way that increases assets without increasing liabilities by an equal amount. For example, the sale of beer may increase cash owed by the customer by $100, whilst only decreasing the inventory value by $60.

### Profit and Loss
The profit and loss statement shows revenue and expenses over a fixed period of time. Lines on the P&L show:
- Total net revenue on goods delivered, after deductions including discounts and rebates. Revenue is recorded when the goods have been delivered, not necessarily when cash is received, as the amount can still exist as accounts receivable. Excise tax and GST are not factored into revenue, as they are collected on behalf of the government.
- Gross profit, which is the net revenue minus the cost of goods sold (COGS) only within that period. Any product not sold remains in the balance sheet as assets.
- Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortisation (EBITDA), which is the gross profit minus administrative expenses not directly related to the manufacture of goods, including operating expenses and salaries for functions outside of Supply Chain.
-  Net profit, which is the earnings minus any interest on loans, depreciation of assets, income tax, etc. Some can be kept as retained earnings and some can be paid as shareholder dividends.

COGS is centered in Supply Chain and includes all the costs of producing and storing a product. It does not include distribution, marketing or sales, as these are captured as operating expenses. It does include:
1. Direct material costs, including all raw materials and packaging materials.
2. Fixed manufacturing costs, including production salaries and production expenses, such as maintenance and utilities.
3. Shutte, the moving of products to warehouse.
4. Warehousing, the storing of products before distribution.

#### Return on Capital Employed
Return on capital employed (ROCE) links the balance sheet and P&L together. It is calculated as the ratio of operating profit to capital employed, as a percentage. Profit comes from the P&L, while capital employed is calculated from the balance sheet, typically as total assets minus short term liabilties. Consider two breweries, each making $10m profit per year. If one does that using $50m worth of inventory (working capital) and equipment (CAPEX) and the other with $100m worth, then the first brewery has a much better (20% vs 10%) ROCE.

### Cashflow Statement
The cashflow statement shows the actual movement of cash entering and leaving the business over a fixed period of time. A negative on a cashflow statement is not necessarily a loss. For example, purchase of raw materials (working capital) or equipment (capital expenditure) become assets on the balance sheet.

#### Managing Cashflow
Growth can put pressure on cash, as a growing brewery needs more working capital and capital expenditure. Cash may get tied up in inventory and accounts receivable. The key drivers for increasing cashflow are thus:
- Financing: such as taking out loans or increasing shareholder equity.
- Performance: reducing overheads through process efficiency and people productivity improvements.
- Procurement: negotiating contracts, finding alternative suppliers, or consolidating purchases.
- Working Capital: optimising inventory, speed up payments from customers, and slow down payments to suppliers.
- Capital Expenditure: assessing upfront costs against estimated savings in the long-term.
- Forecasting: investigating discrepancies regularly and taking corrective action early.

Many of these will be covered in the following sections.

### Bringing the Three Statements Together
#### Working Capital
The brewery buys 1,000 tonnes of malt worth $700k and uses 600 t (60%, $420k) in the first month.

When the inventory is purchased:
- Cashflow Statement will show an immediate flow of cash out equal to -$700k as the supplier is paid.
- Balance Sheet will show -$700k in one short-term asset (cash) and +$700k in another short-term asset (inventory.)
- P&L Statement will show no change.

When the beer is sold:
- P&L Statement will show -$420k as part of the COGS and, say, +$600k as revenue.
- Balance Sheet will show +$600k in accounts receivable, -$420k in inventory, and +$180k in retained earnings, a part of equity.
- Cashflow Statement will show $0 until the customer pays in the invoice.

#### Capital Expenditure
The brewery buys a new centrifuge valued at $1m, that depreciates by $100k per year.

When the product is purchased:
- Cashflow Statement will show an immediate flow of cash out equal to -$1m.
- Balance Sheet will show -$1m in a short-term asset (cash) and +$1m in a long-term asset (equipment)
- P&L Statement will show no change.

As depreciation occurs year on year:
- P&L Statement will show a yearly $100k depreciation expense. It may also show a lower COGS, if the new equipment lowers operational costs (wastage, utilities, maintenance etc.)
- Balance Sheet will change the long-term asset's value from $1m to $900k.
- Cashflow Statement will show no change.

## Standard Costs
### Variable/Fixed and Direct/Indirect Costs
Standard costs are reflected as COGS on the company's P&L when the product is sold. Standard costs can be used in three main ways:
1. Internal pricing; the cost assigned to the beer as it moves through production, as each stage adds more cost (e.g. yeast, packaging materials, more utilities and labour.)
2. Stock valuation; calculating the value of inventory on hand.
3. Assessing performance; by comparing actual costs with the standard costs expected.

Standard costs are classified as variable/fixed (whether or not they scale with production volume) and direct/indirect (whether or not they can be traced to a particular product).

Typically, the standard cost of a product in the brewing department is made up of:
- Material costs, which are often variable, as they change as production volume changes. Raw materials, their standard quantities, and expected yield/losses, all make up the bill of material (BOM) for a product. Since they can be traced to a particular product, they are direct costs.
- Manufacturing costs, which are often fixed, as they mostly stay the same as production volume changes i.e. my salary is unchanged throughout the year. Hourly wages, utilities and maintenance are collected together and allocated using an agreed driver, which is a complex calculation involving production efficiencies, brewery location, shift structures and equipment age. Since they cannot be traced to a particular product, they are indirect costs.

Some costs are a mixture of both, for instance steam for the brewhouse can be variable, as it changes with volume, and indirect, as it can't be allocated to a particular product, instead being averaged over the brew hours.

### Variances to Standard Costs
Actual standard costs and variances are performed by the ERP system (e.g. SAP.) Variances are the differences between actual and expected costs. Variances must be investigated against production and procurement data to determine if they are genuine efficiency improvement opportunities, or if they are a result of price changes, volume or mix effects, or inaccurate master data. 

Production variances occur through:
- Price: the cost of raw materials may change due to seasonality, contracts, exchange rates, freight, surcharges, etc.
- Efficiency: the process may have used more or less material, labour, time, or energy to produce, prompting an investigation on yield, losses, rework, downtime and flowrates.
- Mix: the actual combination of products differs from what was planned, changing the average cost, particularly if some products are more or less expensive to produce than others.
- Volume: if the average manufacturing costs are higher, for example if the same overheads produced less volume due to lowered customer demand.

Brewing is primarily responsible for efficiency, but can also influence price through ingredient selection, mix via sequencing and changeovers, or volume via brew lengths.

#### Accessing Standard Costs
Using SAP, the standard costs of a particular products can be found under the Display Material tile. Enter the material number, select "Costing 2", then Plant 1022, and finally chose the "Current" button.

## Budgets and Forecasts
- The planning cycle in a company starts with the strategic plan, which sets out the mission, vision, and longer-term objectives over 3+ years.
- This feeds the annual operating plan, turning the strategic plan into specific goals (or, KPIs) for the following year.
- Based on these goals, the annual departmental budget can be created. This covers the expected sales and the resources to supply those sales, including working capital, capital expenditure, and manufacturing overheads.
- These form the basis of the standard costing process.

The budget is the approved plan for a period, acting as a target for allocating resources and assessing performance. A forecast if the latest estimate of the full-year result, combining the actual results so far plus expectations for the rest of the year. It is updated based on current information, such as if sales, costs, or plans change. Both can be compared to prior year under comparable periods, which gives historical context.

Any gap between the budget and forecast should be used to understand what changed (efficiency, price, mix, volume) and if possible, any mitigating responses. As an example, for a brief period this year, sugar was more expensive than malt, so the malt/sugar ratio of our beers was tipped in favour of malt. The forecast reflected the actual mix used so far and the expected mix for the rest of the year, including its cost.

### Types of Budgeting
#### Incremental Budgeting
At the end of the year, the brewing manager can start with last year's budget (or actual spend) and add to or subtract from it, according to expected changes in sales, salaries, material costs and utilities prices.

#### Zero Based Budgeting
Each expense must be justified for the new budget period, including what it supports, how much is needed, and why it should be funded. It can be complicated and time-consuming, requiring more documentation, research, justification and training. However, it can eliminate historical expenses that are unnecessary or outdated. 

#### Activity Based Budgeting
Spending estimates are calculated from the resources required to undertake certain activities. The cost is a product of the usage per activity, the quantity per activity, and the price per unit of resource. For instance, the cost of cleaning chemical spend would be the chemical usage per cycle (L/cycle), multiplied by the number of cycles (cycles), multiplied by the price ($/L.)

ZBB and ABB can be used together, for instance ZBB can be used to justify the activity and its purpose, then ABB is used to calculate its budget using usage, quantity and price. Or, incremental budgeting can be used for fixed costs, and ABB for variable costs based on expected demand.

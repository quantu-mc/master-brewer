# Finance and Procurement

## Financial Statements
### Balance Sheet
The balance sheet shows what the company owns (assets) and what it owes (liabilites) as of a particular date.

Assets include:
- Short-term assets (<1 year), including cash, invoices owing from customers, raw materials, and product in process. 
- Long-term assets (>1 year), including equipment and property. The net value of a piece of equipment on the balance sheet will decrease over it's lifespan, with the depreciation, usually per year, accounted for in the P&L statement.

Liabilities are also divided in to short- and long-term, covering loans, debts, bills etc.

It is important to note that the purchase of a new piece of equipment is not recorded as an immediate expense. It will appear as an asset, while the equivalent amount will be either subtracted from the cash reserves (reduced asset) or added to the debt amount (increased liability), with a net equity difference of zero.

Shareholder equity is the difference between assets and liabilities. It is made up of a sum of the initial investment by shareholders, plus the cumulative profit of the business, less any dividends paid to shareholders. Shareholder equity in the company increases when the company conducts business in a way that increases assets without increasing liabilities by an equal amount. For example, the sale of beer may increase cash owed by the customer by $100, whilst only decreasing the inventory value by $60.

### Profit and Loss
The profit and loss statement shows revenue and expenses over a fixed period of time. Lines on the P&L show:
- Total net revenue on goods delivered, after deductions including discounts and rebates. Revenue is recorded when the goods have been delivered, not necessarily when cash is received, as the amount can still exist as accounts receivable. Excise tax and GST are not factored into revenue, as they are collected on behalf of the government.
- Gross profit, which is the net revenue minus the cost of goods sold (COGS) only within that period. Any product not sold remains in the balance sheet as assets.
- Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortisation (EBITDA), which is the gross profit minus administrative expenses not directly related to the manufacture of goods, including operating expenses and salaries for functions outside of Supply Chain.
-  Net profit, which is the earnings minus any interest on loans, depreciation of assets, income tax, etc. Some can be kept as retained earnings and some can be paid as shareholder dividends.

COGS is centered in Supply Chain and includes all the costs of producing and storing a product. It does not include distribution, marketing or sales, as these are captured as operating expenses. It does include:
1. Direct material costs, including all raw materials and packaging materials.
2. Fixed manufacturing costs, including production salaries and production expenses, such as maintenance and utilities.
3. Shutte, the moving of products to warehouse.
4. Warehousing, the storing of products before distribution.

### Cashflow Statement
The cashflow statement shows the actual movement of cash entering and leaving the business over a fixed period of time. A negative on a cashflow statement is not necessarily a loss. For example, purchase of raw materials (working capital) or equipment (capital expenditure) become assets on the balance sheet.

#### Working Capital Example
The brewery buys 1,000 tonnes of malt worth $700k and uses 600 t (60%, $420k) in the first month.

When the inventory is purchased:
- Cashflow Statement will show an immediate flow of cash out equal to -$700k as the supplier is paid.
- Balance Sheet will show -$700k in one short-term asset (cash) and +$700k in another short-term asset (inventory.)
- P&L Statement will show no change.

When the beer is sold:
- P&L Statement will show -$420k as part of the COGS and, say, +$600k as revenue.
- Balance Sheet will show +$600k in accounts receivable, -$420k in inventory, and +$180k in retained earnings, a part of equity.
- Cashflow Statement will show $0 until the customer pays in the invoice.

#### Capital Expenditure Example
The brewery buys a new centrifuge valued at $1m, that depreciates by $100k per year.

When the product is purchased:
- Cashflow Statement will show an immediate flow of cash out equal to -$1m.
- Balance Sheet will show -$1m in a short-term asset (cash) and +$1m in a long-term asset (equipment)
- P&L Statement will show no change.

As depreciation occurs year on year:
- P&L Statement will show a yearly $100k depreciation expense. It may also show a lower COGS, if the new equipment lowers operational costs (wastage, utilities, maintenance etc.)
- Balance Sheet will change the long-term asset's value from $1m to $900k.
- Cashflow Statement will show no change.
